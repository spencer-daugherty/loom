How to make a life plan during a major transition.

A transition plan should stabilize what needs care now, clarify what the next chapter could mean, and create short reviewable actions. It should not require you to predict the rest of your life while the facts are still changing.

By Spencer DaughertyPublished July 30, 2026Updated July 30, 20269 min read

The short answer

Make a life plan during a major transition by documenting current reality, identifying the life areas most affected, writing a temporary direction for the next chapter, choosing one to three 90-day outcomes, breaking them into finite next actions, and scheduling review points. Protect essential maintenance and relationships while leaving uncertain decisions open until better information arrives.

Begin with current reality, not an ideal future

Write what has changed, what remains stable, what decisions are already made, and what is still unknown. A career change, move, separation, new child, caregiving responsibility, graduation, or retirement can alter several life areas at once. Planning becomes clearer when facts and assumptions are separated.

Map the life areas affected by the transition

Consider work, health, relationships, home, money, meaning, learning, and community using your own language. Not every area needs a Goal. Some need protection, some need a specific change, and some can remain in maintenance mode.

Use a six-step transition planning framework

  1. Document the new reality. List constraints, commitments, resources, and open questions without forcing immediate resolution.
  2. Name what matters in this chapter. Write a temporary statement of direction rather than a permanent identity claim.
  3. Choose one to three near-term outcomes. Use a 30- to 90-day horizon where progress can be observed and reviewed.
  4. Create finite Action Plans. Define decisions, conversations, research, paperwork, or practical steps with clear completion points.
  5. Protect basic Little Wins. Keep a few repeatable actions that support stability, connection, or energy.
  6. Schedule review points. Revisit the plan when new information arrives rather than treating revisions as failure.

Example: starting a new role in a new city

A hypothetical person may define three 90-day outcomes: become operational in the new role, create a functional home base, and maintain important relationships during the move.

Action Plans include completing onboarding, identifying key collaborators, setting up utilities, learning essential routes, and scheduling calls or visits. Little Wins might include a short morning walk and one weekly connection with someone important. A 30-day review asks what the new reality has revealed before the next phase is planned.

How detailed should a life plan be during uncertainty?

Detailed enough to guide the next decision, but not so detailed that assumptions masquerade as commitments. Use precise next actions and flexible longer-range direction. Dates belong on obligations and useful checkpoints; uncertain possibilities can stay in Capture or a later list.

What role should reflection play?

Reflection helps distinguish a plan that needs more effort from one based on outdated information. Record what changed, what created energy, what repeatedly created friction, and which assumptions proved wrong. Then update the Goal or Action Plan where the evidence points.

Planning is not professional care

A personal plan can organize decisions and commitments. It is not therapy, legal advice, financial advice, or crisis support. Use qualified professionals when the transition involves needs beyond general planning.
