How to set goals when you don’t know what you want next.

When the destination is unclear, choose a Goal that creates information. A small experiment, conversation, class, prototype, or routine can reveal more than trying to manufacture a permanent answer from an empty page.

By Spencer DaughertyPublished July 30, 2026Updated July 30, 20268 min read

The short answer

When you feel lost, start with direction rather than a permanent destination. Identify the life area that most needs clarity, write what you want more or less of, choose a 30- to 90-day learning Goal, define a small experiment, and schedule Reflection. Use the evidence to continue, revise, or choose a different direction.

Do not demand certainty before taking a useful step

Some Goals pursue a known outcome. Others reduce uncertainty. If you do not know which career direction, community, creative practice, or personal chapter fits, an experiment can produce real evidence about interest, constraints, and next actions.

Start with life areas and signals

Ask which area feels most unclear or neglected. Then list signals without turning them into conclusions: activities that consistently hold attention, responsibilities that feel meaningful, environments that create energy, problems you care about, and constraints that must be respected.

Use this direction-to-evidence framework

  1. Choose one life area. Narrow the search to career, relationships, health, creativity, learning, community, or another personally meaningful area.
  2. Name a direction. Write “more of,” “less of,” and “worth exploring” rather than a final identity.
  3. Create a learning Goal. Define what useful information or experience you want within 30 to 90 days.
  4. Design one small experiment. Choose an action that produces evidence without requiring a permanent commitment.
  5. Protect the experiment on the calendar. Give it enough real time to teach you something.
  6. Reflect on evidence. Record what you learned and decide whether to continue, adjust, or stop.

Example: uncertain about a career change

Instead of setting “find my ideal career,” a hypothetical person creates a 60-day Goal: “Learn whether product research work fits my strengths and preferred working style.”

The Action Plan includes interviewing two practitioners, completing one introductory course module, volunteering for a small research task at work, and writing a Reflection after each experience. The Goal succeeds if it produces a clearer decision—even if the decision is not to pursue that path.

What if every possible Goal feels wrong?

Use a smaller horizon. Choose one week of observation or one conversation. Protect basic responsibilities and avoid creating a large plan merely to escape uncertainty. If feeling lost is persistent, distressing, or affects daily functioning, consider support from an appropriate professional; a planning article or app cannot diagnose the cause.

How do you know whether to commit further?

Review evidence rather than the excitement of setup. Did you want to return to the work? Did the environment fit? What constraints became visible? Did the experiment create curiosity, useful skill, connection, or a clearer “no”? Then choose the next smallest commitment that the evidence supports.

A Goal does not need to define your worth

Goals are tools for directing attention and action. They can change when you learn more. Ending an experiment that answered its question is not failure.

Sources and method

This direction-to-evidence approach is original Loom editorial analysis, not clinical guidance. The American Psychological Association’s overview of building resilience offers general context for adapting during change; it does not prescribe this goal-setting approach.
